I recently listened to [a podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y4JSvu8cM58) featuring [@KrisAbdelmessih](https://x.com/KrisAbdelmessih), a former SIG options trader. He has a [great blog](https://moontower.substack.com) btw. Recommend checking it out.

Early in his career, Kris was a market maker. His job was to buy something, flip it to the next person for a tiny profit, and repeat. It trained his brain to crave smooth, steady, daily wins.

Later, he moved to a bigger fund with real capital and a mandate to hold risk for the long term. But he couldn't shake his old habit. He kept tweaking his models and over-hedging just to make his daily numbers look smooth.

Finally, a mentor asked him a simple question: Do you want to optimize your profits on a daily basis, or something else?

Hedging away every bump feels safe, but it's expensive. If you have a real edge and the bankroll to handle the swings, your job isn't to look good at 4:00PM today. Your job is to tolerate the bumps so you can get paid over the long run.

Listening to Kris, I felt like I was hearing a replay of a chat with my co-founder [@rnikhilcom](https://x.com/rnikhilcom). My co-founder is an ex-poker bro, and we used to compete in mid-stakes house games.

If you judged my poker career by how often I booked a winning session, I looked like a shark. I was profitable in about seven out of every ten sessions. I loved walking away from the table up a little bit.

The problem was that I was actually a breakeven, maybe even slightly losing player. Because I was obsessed with winning every session, I avoided big, swingy spots even when the math was heavily in my favor. I'd play tight to protect a small lead. Then, on the rare night things went wrong, I had no proper stop-loss, so I'd give it all back in one go.

It took my co-founder pointing it out for me to see the flaw: I wasn't playing poker. I was playing a daily ego game.

I was once in a house game with a crazy loose-aggressive player, a guy who normally thrived on variance and once jammed with Q3 just to get me to fold a big hand. I watched this same guy, who chased every profitable spot, fold pocket Jacks against a short-stacked player who went all-in.

At any other point in the night, he would have snapped that call. He had the other guy covered, and the odds were just screaming for a call.

But he folded. Why?

Because he had lost his last few sessions, it was the end of the night, and he was finally sitting at breakeven. He just couldn't stomach the idea of walking out the door red again. In his head, that night was a standalone scoreboard.

That's the trap almost all of us fall into, whether we're trading, playing poker, or building a career. Life is one long, continuous session, but our brains insist on closing the books at the end of every trading session, poker game, or day. We leave long-term upside on the table just to protect a tiny daily win.

Of course, knowing the lesson and living it are two very different things. For all the wisdom my co-founder gave me about zooming out, I've watched him fall into the exact same trap while trading.

He traded the Jensen prediction market a week back. He went big on Jensen not getting fired. Then did a Texas hedge (a "hedge" that doubles the same bet) by going long on Nvidia perps.

When Jensen got fired, he lost big on both. He took the opposite side pre-market. Went short on Nvidia perps with size. Recovered most of it. Then did not exit, Nvidia bounced, and he ended up losing more.

And he has done it enough times for me to know this is something he suffers from too.

Turns out, whether you're folding pocket Jacks to protect a breakeven night, or blowing up a winning trade to erase a red morning, it's the same disease.

We're all just too obsessed with winning today.
