title:  "What Amazon Choosing Robert Pattinson Taught Me About Venture Capital"
---

For more than sixty years, the James Bond franchise had one strict rule: never cast an established star. The audience needed to see the actor as Bond, without any past baggage.

Amazon just broke that rule by casting Robert Pattinson.

Why? Because in a K-shaped economy, nobody wants to take risks anymore.

The movie business used to be a bell curve. You had blockbusters, tiny indies, and plenty of mid-budget hits in between. Today, the middle is dead. In a world of infinite content, where a standard TV show has to compete directly with Reels and TikTok, studios would rather bet $250 million on Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey and hope to make $1 billion.

Video games follow the same math: spend $1 billion building GTA 6 to recoup $3 billion.

Sure, A24 still exists as a studio willing to take weird bets, though even that is a highly differentiated play (the My First Million episode on A24 breaks this down well).

In venture capital, however, there is no A24. Benchmark used to be the closest thing to it, but even they have raised bigger funds. And when everyone is managing huge funds, nobody wants to take a risk on a random founder from Twitter.

When the Applied Compute founder posted a thank-you note to his VCs after his $4.5 billion exit to Palo Alto Networks, the timeline started dunking on him:

* "Industry plant."
* "His VCs basically built the company for him."
* "If I had that kind of backing, I would have sold for $4.5 billion too."

Probably not.

Can you get Bret Taylor, the chairman of OpenAI, to tweet about what a star you were, the way he did for the Instinct founder? If not, you weren't getting that outcome.

Movies, video games, venture capital: every industry is optimizing for legibility to capital.

What critics miss is that the Instinct and Applied Compute founders are the Robert Pattinsons of Silicon Valley. They have pedigrees from Sierra and OpenAI, they're young, and they fit the archetype VCs want to back.

Another test: is your background impressive enough that Lulu can write a viral post about how your team "relaxes" by solving International Mathematical Olympiad problems at 11 PM on a Friday? Your resume is the canvas; it has to be worthy of the mythology VCs and advisors want to paint on it.

This obsession with legibility is also why Indian VCs are rushing to set up shop in the Valley.

They are there to back Indian Ivy League undergrads. They can't compete for the established white founders at a fair price, and multi-country funds are often bound by turf agreements with their US counterparts anyway. They could pay a big premium to buy into consensus US deals just to build a brand (the classic a16z playbook), but the fund math rarely works out.

Instead, the real arbitrage is betting on the Indian founder in the Valley who looks the part, isn't quite as legible to traditional US funds yet, and can still deliver venture returns.

(Of course, it's not just about founder pedigree. As I wrote in my [India AI post](https://manassaloi.com/2025/08/27/india-ai.html), building a software or AI company out of India is playing on legendary difficulty. Read that piece for the challenges.)

VCs have to be legible too. When every trade becomes a consensus trade, it's rational for VCs to pile into the same bets. Founders have to be legible to VCs, and VCs have to be legible to their LPs.

Look back at 2021, when everyone said the Tiger Global playbook was the only way to win, right up until Tiger got wrecked. Today, the consensus playbook is AI. If you're a VC who can't get into the hot AI companies (or at least the ones perceived as hot), you aren't raising your next fund.

Ask any VC friend how their fundraising is going right now. Funds are taking much longer to close. And when survival depends on looking legible to LPs, the safest move is to sit in the same corgi cafe as every other Valley investor, waiting for the next Robert Pattinson to walk through the door.
